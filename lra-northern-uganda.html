The LRA in Northern Uganda: What Happened, and Where Are They Now?

Published: 31 October 2026 | By Vivitek News Desk | Background and explainer

For years, the Lord's Resistance Army (LRA), led by Joseph Kony, devastated communities in northern Uganda. Children were abducted, families were driven from their homes and the conflict spread into neighbouring countries. The movement is now severely weakened, but Kony remains wanted by the International Criminal Court. Here is how the conflict began, what happened, and what is known about the LRA today.

How did the LRA begin?

The Lord's Resistance Army emerged in northern Uganda in the late 1980s. Its rise followed the change of government in January 1986, when Yoweri Museveni's National Resistance Army took power. Northern Uganda, particularly the Acholi sub-region, experienced a period of insecurity, political tension and armed rebellions.

Joseph Kony, a man from the Acholi community who claimed spiritual powers, became the LRA's central leader. The movement drew on elements of Christian language, spiritual beliefs and political claims. Kony said the group sought to replace the government and establish a society guided by the Ten Commandments. Over time, however, its political programme became increasingly unclear while violence against civilians became a defining feature.

It is important to distinguish the LRA from the wider Acholi population. The group claimed to act in the community's name, but it attacked, abducted and terrorised Acholi families and other civilians. Many of the people who suffered most were from the very communities the LRA claimed to represent.

What happened to people in northern Uganda?

The LRA became notorious for attacking villages, trading centres and camps for displaced people. Fighters killed civilians, looted homes and food, burned property, abducted children and adults, and used extreme violence to intimidate communities. Abducted boys were forced into fighting, carrying supplies or other duties. Girls and women were subjected to forced marriage, sexual slavery, domestic servitude and other abuses.

Children were sometimes taken while walking to school, working in gardens or sleeping in their homes. Some abductees were forced to commit violence against other people, including members of their own families. Others escaped, were released or were rescued, but returning home did not automatically end their suffering. Many survivors faced trauma, stigma, interrupted education and difficulties rebuilding family relationships.

The crisis also produced the phenomenon known as “night commuting”. In towns such as Gulu and Kitgum, children left their villages in the evening and walked to hospitals, churches, bus stations and other public places where they hoped to be safer from abduction. They returned home in the morning. For many families, this became a frightening routine.

The scale of displacement was enormous. A United Nations report described around two million people as displaced during the conflict and cited an estimate of about 100,000 conflict-related deaths. Estimates differ between reports and periods, so these figures should be understood as historical estimates rather than an exact final count. Thousands of children were abducted; some humanitarian and research accounts put the number of children abducted over the conflict at around 60,000 or more.

Why did the conflict spread beyond Uganda?

The Ugandan military's pressure on the LRA, the movement of its bases and the collapse of peace efforts pushed the group into remote areas beyond Uganda. By the mid-2000s, LRA forces were operating from areas including Garamba National Park in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), close to what was then southern Sudan.

Peace negotiations known as the Juba peace process took place in 2006–2008, with South Sudanese mediation. The talks produced agreements and raised hopes of an end to the war, but Joseph Kony did not sign the final agreement. In December 2008, Uganda and regional partners launched Operation Lightning Thunder against LRA positions in the DRC. The operation did not capture Kony. LRA units scattered, and attacks on civilians continued in parts of the wider region.

The group subsequently operated across remote parts of the DRC, the Central African Republic (CAR) and areas of what is now South Sudan. Its mobility, difficult terrain, weak state presence and cross-border networks made it difficult for national and international forces to locate and disarm its remaining units.

How did the world respond?

The International Criminal Court (ICC) issued an arrest warrant for Joseph Kony in July 2005, which was unsealed in October that year. The ICC lists 39 counts of war crimes and crimes against humanity against him, relating to alleged crimes in northern Uganda between July 2002 and December 2005.

In 2012, the online campaign film “Kony 2012” brought global attention to the LRA and the effort to arrest its leader. The campaign generated enormous public interest, but it also attracted criticism over its simplified portrayal of a complicated conflict and questions about the best way to support affected communities.

The United States supported regional efforts to find Kony and deployed military advisers to assist the African Union-led effort. These efforts did not result in his capture.

Where are Joseph Kony and the LRA now?

As of 31 October 2026, Joseph Kony has not been publicly confirmed as captured. The ICC continues to list him as “at large”. His precise current location is not independently verified in public information. Researchers have documented the survival of a much smaller LRA remnant in remote borderlands involving the Central African Republic, Sudan and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. These areas are difficult to monitor, and reports about exact locations can quickly become outdated.

The LRA is no longer the large force that terrorised northern Uganda at the height of the insurgency. A 2025 field investigation by researcher Kristof Titeca for The New Humanitarian reported that defections had left Kony with only a small group of fighters, estimated at around a couple of dozen at that time. The same investigation described the group's shift towards survival, small-scale agriculture and cross-border trading networks. This is a reported estimate from 2025, not a verified headcount for 2026.

The same investigation documented the departure of Ali Kony, Joseph Kony's son and a senior figure in the movement. He left the LRA and returned to Uganda in 2023 after travelling through Sudan and South Sudan, including Juba. His return illustrates how some former members have left the movement, although reintegration into ordinary life can be difficult.

What can be stated with confidence? Joseph Kony remains wanted by the ICC and is still listed as at large. The LRA has been severely weakened. Public reporting places remnants in remote Central African borderlands, but there is no independently verified public confirmation of Kony's exact location today.

What has happened in court?

On 6 November 2025, the ICC confirmed all 39 charges against Kony after holding a confirmation-of-charges hearing in his absence in September 2025. This was not a trial or a conviction. The ICC says a trial cannot proceed unless Kony is brought before the Court.

Other former LRA commanders have faced justice. Dominic Ongwen, who was abducted as a child and later became a senior LRA commander, was convicted by the ICC in 2021 of 61 crimes comprising war crimes and crimes against humanity. He was sentenced to 25 years' imprisonment and transferred to Norway in 2023 to serve his sentence.

In Uganda, former LRA commander Thomas Kwoyelo was convicted and sentenced to 40 years in prison in 2024 for crimes including murder, rape, enslavement, torture and kidnapping. These cases are important, but they do not erase the suffering of survivors or resolve every question about accountability.

What remains for northern Uganda?

Northern Uganda has experienced a long period of recovery since the large-scale insurgency ended there. Communities have rebuilt homes, schools, farms and businesses. Yet the effects of abduction, bereavement, sexual violence, displacement and lost education can continue across generations. Survivors may need long-term mental-health support, livelihoods, education, family tracing and recognition of what happened.

The story of the LRA is therefore not only about a fugitive commander. It is also about the people who survived, the children who returned, those born in captivity, families still searching for answers and communities working to rebuild. Any lasting response must put survivors' dignity, safety and recovery at the centre.
