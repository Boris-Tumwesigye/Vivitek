Syphilis: Origin, Causes, Symptoms, Laboratory Diagnosis, Treatment and Prevention

Health | Sexual and Reproductive Health

Syphilis is a sexually transmitted infection caused by a bacterium called Treponema pallidum. It is curable, but without timely treatment it can damage the brain, heart, nerves, eyes and other organs.

1. The Origin and History of Syphilis

The exact geographical origin of syphilis remains a subject of scientific and historical debate. A major epidemic was documented in Europe in the late fifteenth century.

One historical theory links its introduction into Europe to voyages between the Americas and Europe. Another suggests that related infections may have existed in Europe before those voyages. The evidence has not conclusively settled the debate, so no single country or continent can confidently be identified as its proven birthplace.

Today, syphilis occurs worldwide and remains an important public health concern.

2. What Causes Syphilis?

Syphilis is caused by the spiral-shaped bacterium Treponema pallidum.

It mainly spreads through direct contact with an infectious sore during:

  • Vaginal sexual intercourse.
  • Anal sexual intercourse.
  • Oral sexual contact.

The bacterium can enter through moist tissues or small breaks in the skin. An infected pregnant woman can also transmit the infection to her unborn baby through the placenta. This is called congenital syphilis.

Syphilis is not ordinarily spread through sharing toilets, eating utensils, swimming pools, hugging or casual everyday contact.

3. Signs and Symptoms of Syphilis

Syphilis develops in stages. Some infected people may not notice symptoms.

A. Primary Syphilis

The first stage commonly begins around three weeks after infection, although the timing varies.

  • A painless sore called a chancre.
  • A sore on the penis, vagina, cervix, anus, rectum, lips or mouth.
  • Swollen lymph nodes near the affected area.

The sore may disappear without treatment, but the infection remains.

B. Secondary Syphilis

Possible symptoms include:

  • Skin rash, sometimes involving the palms and soles.
  • White or grey patches in the mouth or genital region.
  • Fever and sore throat.
  • Swollen lymph nodes.
  • Headaches and muscle aches.
  • Fatigue and general body weakness.
  • Patchy hair loss in some people.

These symptoms may disappear even though the infection continues.

C. Latent Syphilis

The infection is present but there may be no visible symptoms. Blood tests can still detect evidence of infection.

D. Tertiary Syphilis

Untreated infection can cause serious complications years or decades later. It may affect the heart, blood vessels, brain, spinal cord, nerves, eyes and bones.

Neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis and ear involvement can also occur at different stages, not only during tertiary syphilis.

4. Laboratory Diagnosis of Syphilis

Symptoms alone cannot reliably confirm or exclude syphilis. Laboratory testing is essential.

A. Non-treponemal Tests

  • Rapid Plasma Reagin (RPR).
  • Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL).

These tests detect antibodies associated with the infection. Their quantitative titres help monitor response to treatment. False-positive results can occur, so they should not be interpreted alone.

B. Treponemal Tests

  • Treponema pallidum haemagglutination assay (TPHA).
  • Treponema pallidum particle agglutination (TP-PA).
  • Treponemal enzyme immunoassays (EIA).
  • Chemiluminescence immunoassays (CIA).

These tests detect antibodies directed against the syphilis bacterium. Many people remain positive for years or throughout life even after successful treatment. A positive result therefore does not automatically mean active infection.

Important laboratory point: A presumptive diagnosis generally requires both a non-treponemal and a treponemal test, interpreted alongside the patient's clinical history and previous treatment records.

C. Direct Detection

Where suitable facilities are available, the bacterium may be detected directly from a lesion using specialised microscopy or validated molecular testing such as PCR.

D. Testing for Complications and Pregnancy

Neurological symptoms may require cerebrospinal fluid investigations. Eye or hearing symptoms require specialist assessment.

Syphilis screening during pregnancy is essential because untreated maternal infection can cause miscarriage, stillbirth, premature delivery or congenital syphilis.

5. Treatment of Syphilis

Syphilis is curable with appropriate antibiotics. Penicillin G is the preferred treatment. The dose and duration depend on the stage of infection and whether complications are present.

  • Primary, secondary and early latent syphilis: Benzathine penicillin G, usually 2.4 million units by intramuscular injection once.
  • Late latent syphilis or infection of unknown duration: Benzathine penicillin G, usually 2.4 million units intramuscularly once weekly for three weeks.
  • Neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis or otosyphilis: Requires a different specialist-directed regimen, commonly intravenous aqueous crystalline penicillin G for 10–14 days.

Pregnant women with syphilis should receive penicillin treatment. It is the only treatment with established effectiveness for treating fetal infection and preventing congenital syphilis. Patients reporting penicillin allergy require specialist assessment.

Alternative antibiotics may be considered by clinicians in selected non-pregnant patients with penicillin allergy, depending on the stage and ability to complete follow-up.

Medical warning: These are clinical guideline examples, not instructions for self-medication. Patients should not purchase antibiotics or choose a treatment course without assessment by a qualified healthcare professional.

Sexual partners should be notified, evaluated and treated when indicated. Patients should avoid sexual contact until they and their partners have completed the recommended treatment and a healthcare professional advises that it is safe.

Follow-up blood tests are necessary to assess treatment response and identify possible reinfection.

6. How Can People Prevent Syphilis?

  1. Use condoms correctly and consistently: They reduce risk, although they cannot protect skin areas they do not cover.
  2. Get tested: Seek testing after possible exposure, when symptoms occur or when advised by a healthcare professional.
  3. Attend antenatal care: Pregnant women should be screened early and treated promptly if positive.
  4. Ensure partners are evaluated: Partners of people diagnosed with syphilis should seek medical assessment.
  5. Avoid sexual contact with infectious sores: Direct contact with syphilis lesions can transmit infection.
  6. Complete prescribed treatment: Follow medical advice and attend follow-up appointments.
  7. Do not assume disappearing symptoms mean a cure: Sores and rashes can disappear while infection remains.

7. Why Early Diagnosis Matters

Early detection and treatment help prevent long-term complications and reduce transmission.

Untreated infection during pregnancy can seriously affect the unborn child. Congenital syphilis can cause severe illness, developmental problems and even death.

Healthcare workers, laboratories and antenatal clinics play an important role in identifying infection and ensuring patients receive timely care.

Conclusion

Syphilis is a preventable and curable bacterial infection, but it can become dangerous when diagnosis and treatment are delayed.

Understanding its stages, recognising possible symptoms, using appropriate laboratory tests and completing recommended treatment are essential steps in controlling the disease.

Anyone who suspects exposure should seek confidential medical advice and testing rather than waiting for symptoms to appear.

Health disclaimer: This article is for public health education and does not replace professional medical diagnosis, treatment or advice. Anyone concerned about syphilis should visit a qualified healthcare facility.
